Second-life EV battery hub

Cheap battery—or cheap complete storage system?

Used EV packs combine automotive-grade construction, high power and large capacity. Their purchase price can be compelling. The honest comparison ends at complete £ per usable kWh, not the breaker’s-yard invoice.

Last reviewed: 16 August 2026

High-voltage boundary: Removed EV packs can retain lethal energy after a vehicle is isolated. This guide does not provide pack-opening, interlock-bypass, pinout or live commissioning instructions.

Why people consider them

EV packs offer tens of kWh, high power capability and a potential reuse benefit before recycling. Accident-damaged and end-of-vehicle-life supply can lower battery purchase cost. None of those points establishes condition, safe reuse, inverter compatibility or final economics.

The three costs that matter

Battery £/nominal kWh = purchase price ÷ nameplate capacity
Battery £/usable kWh = purchase price ÷ (nameplate × SOH × selected SOC window)
Complete £/usable kWh = all system costs ÷ estimated usable kWh

Worked estimate A nominal 40 kWh pack at 82% measured SOH, operated through an 80% SOC window, offers an estimated 26.24 kWh DC-usable before inverter losses: 40 × 0.82 × 0.80. At an illustrative £2,000 pack price, battery-only cost is £50/nominal kWh and about £76/usable kWh. If transport, testing, controls, protection, enclosure, inverter and competent installation make the complete project £8,500, the figure becomes about £324/usable kWh. These are arithmetic assumptions—not a live UK quotation.

UK salvage listings are volatile, often omit verified capacity and may carry transport restrictions. No defensible comparable live-price dataset was found for this review, so the structured price table is intentionally empty rather than presenting seller claims as evidence.

Why “40 kWh” is not 40 usable kWh

Original capacity may be gross or usable. Ageing reduces capacity and power; imbalance means the weakest cell group can limit the pack. Temperature, the retained BMS limits, a deliberately narrower stationary SOC window and conversion losses reduce delivered energy further. Capacity SOH alone does not reveal internal resistance, isolation condition or damage history.

Cell chemistry does not establish pack suitability. Mechanical damage, contamination, isolation, sensing, contactors, software limits and enclosure design remain decisive.

Why a pack cannot meet an arbitrary inverter

A supported controller translates battery limits and state to a compatible inverter and may manage contactor sequencing. Voltage range alone is not proof of compatibility. Battery-Emulator documents selected pack/inverter combinations, including Nissan Leaf, Tesla, BMW, MEB, Renault and Hyundai/Kia families; support is model-, firmware- and hardware-specific and remains an engineering project, not a manufacturer warranty.

Pack families worth structured research

The site’s machine-readable family dataset records only defensible overview fields and confidence. The first detailed family is the Nissan Leaf. BMW i3, VW MEB, Renault Zoe, Hyundai/Kia and Tesla remain overview entries until variant-level manufacturer evidence is assembled.

Complete cost checklist

  • pack/modules, transport and traceable provenance;
  • health/capacity and isolation assessment;
  • BMS/controller, CAN interface, contactors and pre-charge;
  • DC fusing, disconnect, busbars, cable and containment;
  • cooling and temperature monitoring where required;
  • compatible inverter/charger, monitoring and electrical work;
  • DNO/export-control work where applicable.
