Battery architecture

Same power, radically different DC current

Low voltage offers a broad DIY ecosystem. High voltage can reduce current, but raises isolation, switching, measurement and competence demands.

Last reviewed: 16 August 2026

“48 V class” does not make a high-current battery harmless. High-voltage DC can be lethal and sustain arcs; this page is conceptual, not a wiring procedure.

Power, voltage and current

Power = voltage × current   ·   Current ≈ power ÷ voltage

48 / 51.2 V

5,000 W ÷ 51.2 V≈ 98 A

Cabling: high-current conductors and terminations
Inverters: broad DIY/rack ecosystem
Isolation: lower voltage, still severe fault energy
Typical use: modular home and off-grid systems

High voltage / EV pack

5,000 W ÷ 360 V≈ 14 A

Cabling: lower current for equal power
Inverters: exact voltage + protocol match
Isolation: HV-rated controls and competence
Typical use: integrated products or specialist reuse

Real current is higher where efficiency is below 100%, and voltage varies with SOC. Cable sizing cannot be derived from current alone: installation method, temperature, grouping, length, voltage drop and protective-device coordination matter.

48/51.2 V systems

A common LFP arrangement is 16 cells in series: 16 × 3.2 V = 51.2 V nominal. Advantages include a mature off-grid/ESS inverter ecosystem, modular rack batteries and easier instrument availability. The penalty is high current at household power, making workmanship and protection critical.

High-voltage systems

Higher DC voltage reduces current and can suit efficient transformerless inverter topologies. It also requires a battery and inverter whose entire operating voltage ranges overlap and whose communications agree. EV packs add OEM contactors, pre-charge expectations, interlocks, isolation monitoring and pack-specific control behaviour.

Compatibility is a matrix

  • minimum/maximum battery and inverter DC voltage;
  • charge/discharge current and power limits across SOC/temperature;
  • CAN or other communications protocol and failure behaviour;
  • pre-charge and contactor ownership;
  • earthing/isolation topology and fault detection;
  • manufacturer or project evidence for the exact combination.

Matching a nominal voltage is necessary but insufficient.

Size energy and power first.
Then choose a voltage architecture that has a defensible compatible system.
