Battery-only tariff shifting

A home battery does not need solar to shift energy

Charge in a defined low-price window and discharge during expensive periods—but include losses, incomplete utilisation, battery wear and fixed costs.

Last reviewed: 16 August 2026

How it works

Cheap overnight grid
Off-peak tariffBattery charging
Conversion lossesStored energy
Usable kWhDaytime home use
Peak import displaced

The inverter/charger schedules charging and later supplies household loads. Solar is optional. A grid-connected battery still falls within DNO connection rules; “no export” settings do not automatically remove notification or approval obligations.

Four checks before payback

Maximum energy charged in window = charge power (kW) × off-peak hours
Peak energy displaced = min(usable battery kWh × discharge efficiency, eligible peak demand)
Input cost per delivered kWh = off-peak rate ÷ round-trip efficiency
Gross saving = delivered kWh × (peak rate − input cost per delivered kWh)
Illustrative off-peak rate7p/kWh
Illustrative peak rate30p/kWh
Delivered after losses8 kWh/day
Potential annual gross benefit≈ £533

Worked example At 90% round-trip efficiency over 300 days, input energy is 8 ÷ 0.90 = 8.89 kWh. Daily input costs about £0.62 and displaced peak electricity £2.40, leaving £1.78 gross. This excludes standby use, degradation/cycle cost, finance, maintenance and export opportunity. Tariffs change: replace both rates and check supplier terms.

Capacity is not enough

Battery capacity tells you how much energy can be stored, while inverter power determines how quickly the battery can be charged or discharged. During a two-hour cheap-tariff window, a 5 kW charger can draw up to 10 kWh from the grid, while a 3 kW charger can draw up to 6 kWh. The amount actually stored will be slightly lower because of charging losses and battery limits.

The inverter also limits how much power the battery can supply to the home at once. For example, if household demand is 7 kW and the inverter can supply 5 kW, the battery provides up to 5 kW and the remaining 2 kW comes from the grid.

Cycle economics

One useful screening method assigns a lifetime energy-throughput cost: battery-system cost allocated to storage ÷ warranted or realistically modelled lifetime delivered kWh. Warranty terms, calendar ageing and retained capacity complicate the estimate. Do not treat every shifted kWh as free after charging.

When the case weakens

  • the peak/off-peak spread is narrow or temporary;
  • the cheap window cannot refill the useful capacity;
  • most household use already falls off-peak;
  • export or flexibility income is assumed but not contractually available;
  • complete installed cost is compared with gross rather than net savings.
