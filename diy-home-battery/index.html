Choose a home battery route that fits your project

Buy a finished system, combine a rack battery and inverter, build a 16S LFP pack or reuse an EV battery. Here is what each route actually involves.

Last reviewed: 16 August 2026

Four ways to approach home storage

The useful question is not simply “which battery is cheapest?” It is how much usable energy and power you get from a complete system, and how much equipment choice you want to handle yourself.

Complete branded battery

A packaged battery and inverter ecosystem, normally supplied and commissioned as one installation.

Voltage: 48 V or high voltage
Capacity: commonly 5–20 kWh
Inverter: integrated, hybrid or AC-coupled
Difficulty: lowest for the owner
Advantage: one supported product
Disadvantage: higher cost and possible lock-in
Suits: buyers prioritising warranty and support

Rack-mount 48/51.2 V LFP

Ready-made modules with an internal BMS, paired with a documented low-voltage inverter.

Voltage: 48/51.2 V
Capacity: often about 5 kWh per module
Inverter: low-voltage battery inverter
Difficulty: medium
Advantage: modular expansion
Disadvantage: communication must be supported
Suits: practical modular builds

DIY 16S prismatic LFP

You choose the cells, BMS, enclosure, fuses and disconnects. It takes more work but gives you control over capacity and parts.

Voltage: 51.2 V nominal
Capacity: about 5–16 kWh per string
Inverter: low-voltage inverter/charger
Difficulty: high
Advantage: transparent, serviceable build
Disadvantage: every supporting part needs choosing
Suits: hands-on builders

Second-life EV battery

A used EV pack can be extremely cheap per kWh, but you need evidence of its remaining capacity and a supported way for the pack and inverter to communicate.

Voltage: usually 300–450 V
Capacity: 20–80+ kWh nominal
Inverter: compatible HV inverter plus controller
Difficulty: advanced
Advantage: large capacity for the purchase price
Disadvantage: pack condition and integration
Suits: experienced reuse projects

Prices move too quickly for a trustworthy permanent range. Use dated quotations in the battery cost comparison; do not let an invented market average choose the winner.

The parts form one chain

Battery
Stores energyBMS / controller
Reports limitsFuse + disconnect

Temperature sensing, current measurement, containment and monitoring support the chain. A BMS watches the battery; it does not replace the main fuse or a correctly rated disconnect.

A large battery does not automatically run a large load. Battery current limits and inverter power set the ceiling. Use the main calculator to estimate the capacity and power your household can use.

Cheap battery or cheap complete system?

Start with the battery price, then add only what that route still needs: BMS or controller, inverter, protection, enclosure, cables, monitoring and installation. A complete EV pack may already contain contactors, current sensing, temperature sensors and a strong enclosure, so do not price those twice. Equally, loose LFP cells are not yet a working home battery.

Complete-system £/usable kWh = battery and supporting equipment ÷ estimated usable capacity

Let the numbers decide. A second-life EV system can remain remarkably inexpensive after integration, while a rack system may win where labour, warranty and expandability matter more.

Keep the boundary clear: Planning the architecture and choosing low-voltage parts are different from connecting storage to household mains. Removed EV packs can retain lethal voltage; opening packs, defeating interlocks and live high-voltage work are outside this guide.
